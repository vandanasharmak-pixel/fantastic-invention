Deliverable One
# The Simulation, End to End
This is not three role-plays wearing a costume. It is one client, one account, one deteriorating relationship, revealed across five episodes — and fifteen people who will spend two hours discovering that the frameworks they were taught are much harder to use when a real person is disappointed in them.
## What this Lab actually is
Your participants sat through a strong, framework-rich session. They can now recite the Difficult Conversation Lifecycle — Prepare, Regulate, Deliver, Recover. They can define STOP and ARC. They can tell you the difference between a Position and an Interest, between a Fact and a Story. They understand Identity, Emotion, Risk, Assumptions, Pressure, Relationship History, Future Consequences, Accountability Language, Curiosity, Escalation and Recovery. They understand these ideas the way you understand how to swim after watching a documentary about swimming.
The Practice Lab exists to put them in the water. Its single job is conversion — turning recognition into reflex. Knowledge that a person can explain is not the same as behaviour a person will perform when a client is cold, the information is incomplete, and their own competence is being quietly questioned. The gap between those two things is exactly where client relationships are lost, and it is the only thing this Lab is designed to close.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this Lab, a participant will have done the following — not understood them, done them — at least once, under observation, with consequences:
Separated fact from story in real time, out loud, while under mild social pressure, and noticed how often their first interpretation of a client’s behaviour was a story they had mistaken for a fact.
Surfaced an interest behind a stated position, both their own and the client’s, and experienced how a conversation changes shape the moment the interest is named.
Regulated themselves before responding at least once when the instinct was to defend, explain or reassure — and felt the physical difficulty of doing so.
Used curiosity as a tool rather than a personality trait — asking a question whose purpose was to change the conversation, not to fill silence.
Made an escalation decision under uncertainty — choosing to escalate, or choosing not to — with incomplete information and lived with the result.
Protected a relationship while delivering unwelcome news, holding accountability language and honesty together rather than trading one for the other.
Repaired trust after damaging it, in a replay, and observed the specific behaviours that moved the relationship in each direction.
Received observation and coaching from peers without defensiveness and given it without cruelty — the two halves of a team that can raise its own standard.

## The design philosophy
Seven principles hold this Lab together. If you ever have to choose between following the script and honouring one of these principles, honour the principle.
### One continuous client, not scattered scenarios
Participants inherit a single strategic account — Cirrus Systems — and they hold it for the entire Lab. Because it is one account, every decision compounds. A story someone accepted as fact in Episode One becomes the reason a meeting goes wrong in Episode Two. An escalation someone avoided in Episode Three arrives, uninvited and larger, in Episode Four. This continuity is what makes the room feel like a series rather than a set of exercises, and it is what makes consequence — the deepest teacher in the room — possible at all.
### Progressive disclosure: nobody ever has the whole picture
Information is released deliberately, a little at a time, and — critically — unequally. Different tables receive different confidential cards. This means Table A may be certain of something Table B has never heard, and both are partly right and partly blind. This is not a trick. It is the actual condition of client work: you are always deciding with less than you wish you had, and the person across the table knows things you do not. The discomfort this creates is the curriculum.
### Learning by consequence, not by correction
When a participant makes a poor move, you do not correct it in the moment. You let the client react the way a real client would, and you let the participant feel the temperature of the room change. Consequence teaches faster and deeper than correction, and it teaches without wounding, because the lesson comes from the situation rather than from the authority figure at the front.
### Deliberate practice on the moments that matter
Most of a difficult conversation is ordinary. The relationship turns on a handful of seconds — the sentence right after the client says something sharp, the decision to ask one more question or to move on, the choice to escalate or absorb. The Lab isolates these moments and, through Replay (Deliverable Ten), lets participants practise the same critical seconds more than once. Reps on the pivot points, not on the padding.
### Psychological safety as the precondition for everything
People will not take the risks this Lab requires — trying a harder move, failing in front of peers, being observed — unless the room is safe. You build that safety in the first eight minutes, and you protect it ruthlessly for the next hundred and twelve. A room that laughs at a clumsy attempt will never produce a brave one. Your job is to make failure the most normal thing in the room.
### The facilitator coaches; the room teaches
The insight in this Lab does not come from you. It comes from participants observing each other, and from the debrief conversations you host. You are not the source of wisdom; you are the person who asks the question that lets the room find its own. This is cognitive apprenticeship and guided discovery, and it asks you to talk far less than your instinct wants you to.
### Repetition without humiliation
The Lab repeats — the same account, similar pressures, escalating stakes — so that a behaviour tried once in Episode One can be tried better in Episode Four. Repetition is how behaviour sets. But it only works if each repetition is emotionally survivable. The design gives everyone many attempts and no verdicts.

## Facilitator preparation
Prepare in this order. Each step assumes the one before it.
#### Two days before
Read the entire dossier (Deliverable Two) as if you were a new VEE account lead being briefed on Cirrus. You must know this client cold — the names, the history, the politics — because participants will ask you questions in character and your hesitation breaks the illusion instantly.
Read all twenty confidential cards (Deliverable Four) and, crucially, note which card contradicts which. The Lab’s tension lives in these contradictions; if you do not hold the map of them, you cannot steer the debriefs to the moments that matter.
Decide who plays the client. If you have a second coach, they play the client voice throughout — using the scripts in Deliverable Five — which is far stronger than the facilitator switching roles. If you are solo, you play the client, and you must rehearse the transitions between "facilitator voice" and "client voice" so the room can always tell which one is speaking.
#### One day before
Print and collate every pack (printing instructions below). Assemble the confidential envelopes and label them by table and episode. This is fiddly; do not leave it for the morning.
Draw the Trust Meter (Deliverable Nine) on the whiteboard or prepare the flipchart version. Set the needle to its starting position and leave it visible from the first minute.
Walk the room layout in your mind. Where do the three tables sit? Where do observers stand during a live conversation? Where is the client "seat"? Rehearse the physical choreography of Episode Two, because a live client conversation with fifteen people watching needs a clear stage or it dissolves into a murmur.
#### The morning of
Lay out each table’s Prologue pack face down. Set the sealed episode envelopes at the facilitator table, sorted by episode then by table. Put spare markers and sticky pads on every table.

## Room layout
The physical room does real pedagogical work. Get it right and the Lab half-runs itself; get it wrong and you will fight the furniture for two hours.
Three round or square tables, five chairs each, spread wide enough that a raised voice at one table does not leak into another. Confidential means confidential; tables must not overhear each other’s cards.
A clear front "stage" — simply an open area with two chairs facing each other and a third chair or standing spot for the facilitator. This is where every live client conversation happens, in view of the whole room. Do not let live conversations happen at the tables; the whole room must witness them for the observation and debrief to work.
The Trust Meter on the main wall, large, central, permanently visible. It is the emotional scoreboard of the Lab and it must never be out of sight.
A "War Room" flipchart per table — each team’s working wall, where their stakeholder map, their facts-versus-stories, and their decisions live. These become the raw material of the Grand Debrief.
An observer zone — a short arc of chairs or a standing line beside the stage, close enough to read faces, far enough not to intrude. Observers rotate; everyone observes at least once.

## Learning transfer principles
A Lab that produces insight in the room and no change on Monday has failed. Five principles govern whether the behaviour survives contact with real clients.
#### Behaviour is rehearsed, not resolved
Participants leave with reps in their body, not resolutions in their head. That is why the Lab makes them perform, replay, and perform again. Resolutions decay in days; rehearsed behaviour, especially rehearsed under emotional load, persists for months.
#### The rule they wrote, they keep
Every debrief ends with participants articulating their own rule — "When a client goes quiet, I will ask before I explain." Because they authored it from their own experience, it has the force of a discovery rather than an instruction. You will find these rules on their Learning Log pages; the Lab is engineered to fill those pages with self-authored principles.
#### The account is a mirror
Cirrus is fictional, but every participant recognises a client of their own in it. Your debriefs constantly ask "Where have you seen this?" so that the fictional account becomes a lens on their real portfolio. Transfer happens the moment a participant stops talking about Cirrus and starts talking about their own client without noticing they switched.
#### Peers as the enforcement mechanism
Because they practised together and coached each other, participants leave with shared language and shared witnesses. A team that watched each other try the harder move will hold each other to it afterwards far more reliably than any manager could.
#### One commitment, not ten
The close asks for a single, specific, behavioural commitment — not a list. Ten intentions produce zero changes; one concrete commitment, spoken aloud to peers, produces one real change, which is the most any two-hour Lab can honestly promise.

## Success criteria
You will know this Lab worked — not from a happy sheet, but from what you see and hear. Watch for these signals.
--- TABLE ---
|  | WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE / By the Grand Debrief, participants are talking about their own real clients unprompted, using the language of fact-versus-story and interest-versus-position as if it were their own. / At least once, a participant says some version of "that has happened to me" — the sign the fiction has become a mirror. / The room self-corrects: someone tries a defensive move and a peer, not you, gently names it. / Participants disagree with each other about what the right move was, and the disagreement is productive rather than combative — a sign the ambiguity landed and safety held. / Every participant leaves with one written, specific, behavioural commitment on their Action Planner page, and can say it out loud to the room without hedging. / The energy at the close is reflective rather than exhausted — people are quiet and thoughtful, not relieved it is over. |

If most of those are true, the Lab has done its work: the frameworks the participants recited this morning have started, in a handful of hard little moments, to become the way they behave when a client is unhappy. That is the whole ambition. Everything that follows in this guide exists to make it happen.

## The client: who Cirrus is
Cirrus Systems is a fast-scaled enterprise software company whose cloud platform runs supply-chain and logistics operations for large manufacturers, retailers and third-party logistics firms. Founded eleven years ago, it grew quickly, took a major growth-equity investment three years ago, and is now being groomed for an IPO. Its platform sits in the critical path of its customers’ operations — when Cirrus is down, warehouses and delivery networks feel it — which means its customers are demanding, security-conscious, and unforgiving of instability.
Cirrus’s identity matters to how it behaves as a client. It is an engineering-proud, investor-pressured, reliability-anxious organisation. Its senior engineers hold real power and are deeply skeptical of outside vendors, having been promised transformation before and delivered disruption. Its finance function is under permanent pressure because the path to IPO demands both growth and a credible march toward profitability. And its board, having recently installed a new CEO to get the company public-ready, is impatient for a visible sign that the platform can scale into its next chapter. These three forces — engineering caution, investor anxiety, board impatience — pull in different directions, and VEE sits in the middle of all three.
### Business model in one paragraph
Cirrus earns recurring subscription revenue from enterprise customers who run mission-critical logistics on its platform, with expansion revenue as customers adopt more modules and more seats. Its strategic problem is that the original platform — a monolith built fast in the early years — cannot scale to the size, multi-tenancy and reliability that both its largest customers and its IPO story now require. This is the strategic reason Cirrus needs a re-architected, cloud-native platform in the first place: you cannot take a company public on infrastructure the market believes will fall over. VEE’s programme is therefore not an IT project to Cirrus’s leadership. It is the technical foundation of the company’s next valuation. That is why the stakes are higher than the contract value suggests, and why the politics are sharper than a technology programme would normally attract.

## The transformation: Programme "Cirrus One"

The original platform grew fast and organically, and inherited a tangle of technical debt — a monolithic core, a single shared database straining under multi-tenant load, and a scatter of services bolted on over the years that make every change slow and every incident wide. The result is that Cirrus cannot scale to its largest prospects or promise the uptime the market expects. Cirrus One exists to fix that.

## Known personalities — the character notes
These notes are gold for whoever plays the client. Read them as character briefs, not bullet points. Each person wants something, fears something, and has a history that explains both.
#### Alan Reyes — VP Engineering & Chief Architect
The most important person in this account, and the one whose trust is slipping. Reyes is a deeply experienced engineer who has shipped at scale and been burned doing it. Two years ago, before VEE, he championed a different vendor who sold Cirrus an "AI-powered automated migration" — a dazzling demo, real money, and a tool that never worked in production and cost the company the better part of a year. Reyes carried that internally and was left exposed when it failed. Then, ten months into the VEE engagement, the platform suffered a major production outage during a peak period; customers escalated, and Reyes took the heat in front of the board. He does not want to be surprised ever again. His caution is not obstruction; it is scar tissue. Win his trust by being relentlessly straight with him, and he becomes VEE’s strongest advocate. Reassure him without evidence, and you confirm his worst fear — that VEE is another vendor who will smile at him right up until it fails. His private motto, which a colleague has heard him say more than once: "Bad news early is a conversation. Bad news late is a post-mortem."
#### Marcus Webb — Chief Financial Officer
Webb is the reason the money is tight and the reason the change request is stuck. Brought in alongside the new CEO to get Cirrus IPO-ready, he has imposed a hiring freeze and a spend discipline that has rippled through every team. He is numerate, unsentimental, and allergic to anything described as "strategic" without a number attached. He is not anti-VEE; he is anti-undefended-spend. He holds CR-114, a $1.2M change request, on his desk not because he has decided against it but because nobody has given him a value story he could defend to the board. Reach him with evidence and a defensible return, and he moves. Reach him with enthusiasm, and he closes the door.
#### Dana Coetzee — Chief Executive Officer
Eighteen months into the role, installed by the investors to make Cirrus public-ready. Dana barely knows VEE exists as a distinct entity — to her, VEE is "the platform vendor." She has made one consequential move that now shapes everything: she has promised the board she will showcase the Atlas platform launch at the Q3 review, eight weeks out. That promise is now a gravitational force pulling on the whole account. Dana is not unreasonable, but she is exposed, and exposed executives want certainty. The temptation she creates — to give her the confident date she wants — is precisely the trap the Lab is built around.
#### Priya Nadkarni — VP Platform Engineering (your sponsor)
VEE’s champion and the reason the account exists in its current form. Priya fought internally to bring VEE in and has staked real credibility on the programme. She is warm, capable, loyal, and increasingly stretched. She reports to Webb, which means she is defending VEE to the person least convinced of its value, and she owns Osei’s infrastructure team, which means the environment delay lives in her house. She is the ally to protect — and, as the cards will reveal, she is more exposed than she is letting on. Handle her well and she spends her capital for VEE; handle her badly, or over her head, and VEE loses its only real friend in the building.
#### Susan Okafor — Director, Engineering Enablement
The quiet power in adoption. Susan runs the enablement function and holds informal authority over whether Cirrus’s own engineering teams actually adopt the new platform or route around it. A group of her engineers were burned by a workflow change during Phase 1 and she will not expose them to another disruptive cutover without being sure. Publicly she is cautious; privately, as the cards reveal, she is more winnable than she appears — she has simply never been properly engaged. She decides, in practice, whether the launch lands softly or hard.
#### Raymond Osei — Director, Cloud Infrastructure & SRE
The man in the impossible position. Prologue · 10 minutes
## Inheriting the Account
### Scenario
The room does not yet know it has a problem. Your only jobs in these ten minutes are to make the room safe, hand over the account with real weight, and start the Trust Meter. Everything after this depends on getting the emotional temperature right now. Do not rush it to buy time later; a rushed prologue costs you far more than the minute it saves.
### Facilitator script — word for word
▸ Stand at the front. Wait until the room is genuinely quiet before you speak. The first silence you hold teaches them that silence is allowed in here.
“This morning you learned a set of frameworks. STOP. ARC. Fact versus story. Position versus interest. You can all define them. I know you can, because you just spent two and a half hours on them. So I’m not going to ask you to define anything today.”
“Today we find out something more interesting — and, honestly, more uncomfortable. We find out what happens to those frameworks when a real person is disappointed in you, when you don’t have all the information, and when the relationship you’re responsible for is quietly starting to slip.”
“Because here is the thing nobody tells you about difficult conversations. The hard part was never understanding them. The hard part is doing the right thing in the three seconds after someone says something that stings. That’s what we’re going to practise. Not the theory. The three seconds.”
▸ Pause. Let that land. Then shift your tone — warmer, more direct.
“Two ground rules, and only two. First: in this room, being clumsy is completely fine. You are going to try things that don’t work. That is the point. Nobody here is being assessed, ranked, or reported on. The only way to do this badly is to play it safe. Second: what happens in the room stays in the room. If someone fumbles, we learn from it together and we never repeat it outside. Can I have that from everyone? A nod is enough.”
▸ Wait for the nods. Actually wait. Then hand over the account.
“Right. You are no longer fifteen people on a training course. As of this moment, the three of you at each table are the VEE delivery leadership on one of our most important clients. The relationship is two years old. It’s worth eighteen million dollars. And it has just landed on your desk, because the person who used to run it has moved on — and her handover was, let’s say, rushed.”
▸ Hand each table its bound dossier. Let them feel the weight of it. Do not let them open it yet.
“This is your account file. Everything you know about this client is in here — the history, the people, the politics, the things that have gone right and the things that have gone wrong. In a moment I’m going to give you six minutes to read it, the way you’d read a real handover file on a Monday morning when you’ve inherited something you didn’t build. Read for the relationship, not just the facts. Notice what worries you. One more thing before you open it —”
▸ Gesture to the Trust Meter on the wall.
“This is the client’s trust in us. Right now it’s sitting here — decent, but not what it was. It moves. Everything you do today moves it, up or down, and you’ll see it move. Nobody’s keeping score against you. But the client is always keeping score. That’s not a game. That’s the job. Open your files.”

Read the dossier for six minutes. Read for the relationship, not only the facts.
On sticky notes, each person writes the one thing that worries you most about this account. Stick them on your table’s War Room flipchart.
As a table, agree in ninety seconds: who is our closest ally, and who is our biggest risk? Write both on the flipchart.
Do not open any envelope. Envelopes open only on the facilitator’s cue.
### Expected participant behaviours
Most tables will fixate on the schedule slippage (R-01) and the money (CR-114) — the hard, countable risks — and under-weight the relationship signals (Reyes’ email, the cooling, the unmet Okafor).
Some will spot Anika’s buried email; most will skim past it. Note who reads carefully.
A strong table will name Reyes or Okafor as the biggest risk. A weaker table will name "the timeline."

Episode One · 18 minutes
## Something Feels Wrong
### Scenario
Reyes’ email (Email 3 in the dossier) arrives — or rather, you deliver it now as the episode’s opening move. It is not a complaint. It is worse: it is a quietly worried senior engineer saying he no longer trusts "on track" without evidence. There is no crisis to manage. There is only a faint, deniable signal — the kind that a busy team explains away. The episode asks one deceptively hard question: is there even a problem here, and what is fact versus story about it?
### Story progression
This is the episode where assumptions are laid down that later episodes will detonate. Whatever story the teams tell themselves now about "what Reyes really means" and "whose fault the slippage is" becomes the ground they stand on in Episode Two — and the ground that gives way in Episode Three. Your job is to let them commit to a story, gently, so that the later collision is real.
### Facilitator script — word for word
▸ Hold up a printed copy of Reyes’ email, or reveal it on the flipchart. Read it aloud in Reyes’ voice — measured, not dramatic. Then return to facilitator voice.
“That landed in your account inbox three days ago. It’s from Alan Reyes — VP of Engineering and Chief Architect. If you read your dossier, you know exactly who he is and exactly what history sits behind that email. If you skimmed it — well, you’re about to wish you hadn’t.”
“Here’s your task, and I want you to resist the very first thing your brain wants to do. Your brain wants to jump to "what do we reply?" Don’t. Before you decide what to do, you have to decide what’s actually true. So, at your table, split this email into two columns on your flipchart. On the left: the facts. What do you actually know? On the right: the stories. What are you inferring, assuming, or filling in? Be honest with yourselves. Most of what feels like fact is going to be story. You have six minutes. Go.”
### Participant instructions
On your War Room flipchart, draw two columns: FACT and STORY.
Take Reyes’ email apart. Every statement goes in one column. Argue about the borderline ones — the argument is the point.
Then, underneath, answer three questions in one line each: What does Reyes actually want (his interest)? What is he afraid of? What is the smallest first move that would settle him?
Choose one person to speak your table’s answers. You have six minutes for the columns, then we compare.
### Expected participant behaviours
Teams will initially file too much under FACT. "He’s losing confidence in us" will be written as a fact; it is a story. Watch for it — it is the richest teaching moment in the episode.
Stronger teams will locate the interest quickly ("he’s afraid of being surprised in front of his engineers and the board") because they read the dossier and connected the outage history.
Weaker teams will jump to solutions ("we’ll send a detailed status report") without touching the interest — solving the position, not the person.
### Likely mistakes

|  | COMMON PARTICIPANT ERRORS / Story-as-fact. The commonest error by far: writing an interpretation in the FACT column. Assign one observer per table for this episode (rotate later). Give them the Fact-vs-Story observation sheet (Deliverable Seven). ▸ As they read, gently test the softest one: "Is that a fact, or is that what you think it means?" Do this two or three times, warmly, until the room starts catching its own stories. Do not do it to every item — you are teaching a reflex, not running an audit.

▸ Wait. Someone will own one. Thank them visibly — this is the behaviour you want the room to see rewarded.

▸ Steer toward the interest: he is afraid of being surprised in front of customers and the board again, because of the outage. Let a participant say it. If nobody does, ask: "What happened the last time things went quiet with him?" — the dossier gives them the answer.

### Learning points (let them articulate these)
Most of what feels like fact is story; acting on story as if it were fact is how relationships get misread.
Under a stated position ("I don’t have a clear picture") sits an interest ("don’t let me be surprised") and a fear (the last outage).
The reassurance reflex answers the position and abandons the person.
### Transition
“So you’ve worked out — roughly — what’s true and what he’s really afraid of. Good. Now you have to do the hardest thing in this whole business. You have to actually talk to him. He’s agreed to meet. He’s sitting down. Let’s go.”

Episode Two · 22 minutes
## The First Client Meeting
### Scenario
The first live conversation. One participant — the "lead" — sits across from Reyes (played by the coach, or by you) at the front of the room while everyone watches. Reyes is not angry. He is quiet, careful, and unconvinced, and he has come to find out whether VEE will be straight with him. The scripted client behaviour (Deliverable Five) escalates only if the participant reaches for reassurance; it softens if the participant leads with curiosity and ownership. The room watches a colleague try to do live what they all analysed in Episode One.
### Story progression
This episode is where the story the teams built in Episode One meets a real person. If they decided "reassure him," they will watch that decision fail in real time. If they found his interest, they will watch the temperature in the room change when the participant names it. Either way, the account moves — and the Trust Meter moves with it, visibly, for the first time.
### Facilitator script — word for word
“We’re going to do something that takes a bit of nerve, so let me make it safe first. One person is going to sit down with Reyes and have the opening of this conversation. Not the whole thing — just the first few minutes, the part that actually decides everything. Everyone else watches, and we’re watching to learn, never to judge. When it’s done, the person in the chair gets to speak first about how it felt. Nobody critiques before they do.”
“And here’s the safety net: at any point, either the person in the chair or I can call "hold." When we say hold, we freeze, we talk about what’s happening, and — this is the good part — you can rewind and try the moment again. You are never trapped in a bad conversation in this room. Real life doesn’t give you that. We do.”
▸ Ask for a volunteer, or select someone whose table did strong work in Episode One. Seat them across from Reyes. Take a breath. Then, in Reyes’ voice, open the conversation using Deliverable Five, Episode Two scripts.
“(as Reyes) Thanks for coming in. I’ll be honest — I almost didn’t ask for this meeting. I didn’t want to be the difficult one. But I’ve been doing this a long time, and something’s not sitting right with me on the Atlas launch. So. Tell me where we really are.”
▸ Now the participant responds. Follow the branching in Deliverable Five: if they reassure, Reyes cools; if they ask a real question or own something honestly, Reyes opens. Run the beat for three to four minutes. Call "hold" at the first genuinely pivotal moment — the first reassurance, or the first strong question — and turn to the room.
### Participant instructions
One lead sits with Reyes and runs the opening of the conversation. Your table may pass you one sticky-note "lifeline" during a hold if you are stuck — one word or phrase, no speeches.
Everyone else observes against your sheet: what did the lead do right before the client warmed or cooled?
When the facilitator calls hold, the lead speaks first — "what was I trying to do, and what happened?" — before anyone else comments.
### Expected participant behaviours
The lead will very likely reassure at least once ("we’re confident we’ll hit the date"). This is gold. Let it happen and let Reyes cool.
Under the pressure of being watched, even strong analysts revert to defending. The gap between their Episode One insight and their Episode Two behaviour is the single most powerful thing the Lab reveals. Name it in the debrief.
A skilled lead will ask Reyes what would settle him, or will own the communication gap ("you’re right that we’ve gone quiet, and that’s on us"). Watch Reyes visibly soften — and make sure the room watches too.
### Likely mistakes
--- TABLE ---
|  | COMMON PARTICIPANT ERRORS / The reassurance spiral. "We’re on track, we’re confident, don’t worry." Each reassurance without evidence lowers trust further, and Reyes — per the script — gets quieter and cooler, which panics the lead into more reassurance. Let this spiral run just long enough for the room to feel it, then call hold. / Defending the timeline instead of owning the silence. The lead argues about integration complexity. Reyes doesn’t care about complexity; he cares about being kept in the dark. The lead is answering a question Reyes didn’t ask. / Data-dumping. Burying Reyes in status detail to seem thorough. It reads as hiding behind information. Reyes wants candour and contact, not a dashboard. / Over-owning / grovelling. The opposite failure — collapsing into apology. Reyes respects ownership, not self-flagellation. Watch for the lead who over-corrects into weakness. |
--- END TABLE ---
### Observer guidance
This is the Lab’s first major observation moment. Observers use the full Deliverable Seven sheet, but you point them at one behaviour above all: the pivot. "Find the exact sentence where the client warmed or cooled, and write down the words the lead used just before it." That single instruction produces the most useful debrief evidence you will get all day.
### Replay (built in — 3 to 4 minutes)
--- TABLE ---
|  | COACHING NOTES / When you call hold on a reassurance, do not fix it. Ask the lead: "What were you trying to do just then?" (Answer, almost always: "reassure him / calm it down.") Then: "And what did his face do?" Then the money question: "If the goal isn’t to reassure him — what is it?" Let them find "to show him I’ll be straight with him." Then: "Rewind. Same moment. Try it that way." Let them replay the exact beat. The room watches the same person do the same moment better. This is deliberate practice made visible, and it is the most persuasive thing that happens in the Lab. See Deliverable Ten for the full replay method. |
--- END TABLE ---
### Timing
0:00–0:02 — Set up safety and the hold/replay mechanic.
0:02–0:07 — Live conversation, beat one.
0:07–0:11 — First hold; the lead reflects; replay the pivotal moment.
0:11–0:16 — Continue or run a second lead from a different table on the same opening.
0:16–0:22 — Debrief and transition.
### Debrief — facilitator questions
“(to the lead, first, always) Before anyone else says a word — how did that feel, and what were you trying to do?”
“(to the observers) Who caught the exact moment Reyes warmed or cooled? Read me the sentence the lead used right before it.”
▸ This is where the learning crystallises. When an observer reads back "and right after they said ‘we’re confident,’ Reyes went quiet" — the whole room sees cause and effect. Let it sit. Do not explain it.
“So here’s the uncomfortable one. In Episode One, at your tables, most of us worked out exactly what Reyes was afraid of. And then, in the chair, under a bit of pressure, what did we reach for instead?”
▸ Let them say it: reassurance / defending / the safe move. This is the Lab’s central revelation — that knowing the framework and using it under pressure are different skills. Do not soften it, but do not moralise. Just let the room notice.
### Update the Trust Meter
--- TABLE ---
|  | FACILITATOR DEBRIEF / Move the needle live, based on what actually happened. If the lead reassured and Reyes cooled, drop it and say why. If the lead owned the silence and asked what would settle him, raise it and say why. Narrate the logic every time: "It moved down here — not because we lied, but because we answered his fear with comfort instead of evidence. That’s the whole lesson of the day, and we just watched it happen." |

### Learning points
Insight does not survive pressure automatically; behaviour under pressure is its own skill and must be rehearsed.
Curiosity and ownership warm a wary client; reassurance and defence cool them.
The relationship turns on single sentences — the "three seconds" from the Prologue, now made concrete.
### Transition
“You’ve had your first real conversation with him. Maybe it went well, maybe it wobbled — either way, you were working with what you knew. But you didn’t know everything. Nobody in this room knows everything. And in the next few minutes, some things you didn’t know are going to arrive — and not everyone’s going to get the same news.”

Episode Three · 20 minutes
## Hidden Truths
### Scenario
The confidential envelopes open. This is the episode where progressive disclosure does its work: each table receives different cards, so each table suddenly knows something the others do not — and discovers that a story it committed to in Episode One was wrong. Table A learns the delay is caused by Cirrus’s own hiring freeze. Table B learns the sponsor may be about to lose her seat. Table C learns a competitor is circling Phase 3. None of them has the whole picture, and all of them must now decide what to do with a partial truth — including whether, and how, to share it.
### Story progression
Episodes One and Two were about a signal and a conversation. Episode Three breaks the account open. The assumptions the teams made — "the delay is our integration problem," "Priya is our stable sponsor," "Phase 3 is ours" — are each contradicted by a card somewhere in the room. The learning is visceral: I acted confidently on something I did not actually know. This is also where the buried email (Cards 2 and 7) can be discovered, reframing blame into a shared, fixable problem.
### Facilitator script — word for word
“Every one of you made decisions in the last half hour based on what you knew. That’s all any of us can ever do. But here’s the quiet truth of client work: you never know everything, the person across the table knows things you don’t, and the colleague at the next table is holding a piece of the picture you’d give anything to see. So let’s find out what you didn’t know.”
▸ Distribute the sealed Episode Three envelopes — different cards to different tables, per the routing sheet in Deliverable Four. Do not let tables see each other’s cards yet.
“Open them. Read what’s inside carefully — this is real, it’s true, and it changes things. Then, at your table, answer two questions. One: what did we get wrong? Be specific — what did we treat as fact that this card just contradicted? Two: does this change what we should have said to Reyes? You’ve got five minutes. When you’re done, we’re going to do something interesting — we’re going to see whether you tell each other.”
### Participant instructions
Open your envelopes and read every card your table receives. Assume all of it is true.
On your flipchart, under a new heading WHAT WE GOT WRONG, write the specific assumption each card overturns.
Decide: does this change the move with Reyes — and with anyone else?
Then the cross-table moment: you will have two minutes where tables may — if they choose — share what they’ve learned with the room. You are not required to. Decide, as a table, what you share and what you hold, and why.
### Expected participant behaviours
The reframe lands hardest on tables that were most confident in Episode One. The team that blamed "our integration work" for the delay will visibly recalibrate when they read that Cirrus’s freeze is the true cause.
The cross-table sharing moment splits the room: some tables share openly (relationship instinct), some hold their card as advantage (competitive instinct). Both are real client behaviours; do not pre-judge them.
A strong table will connect its card to a concrete change in the Reyes conversation — "now we can tell him the truth about the environments, and offer to fix it together."
### Likely mistakes
--- TABLE ---
|  | COMMON PARTICIPANT ERRORS / Weaponising the truth. Discovering the delay is Cirrus’s fault and reaching for it as a defence ("see, it wasn’t us"). Being right about blame is the fastest way to lose the relationship. Watch for the table that gets defensive-relieved rather than constructive. / Hoarding the card. Treating a piece of information as leverage to be kept rather than shared. Sometimes correct, often relationship-damaging. Make the choice conscious in the debrief rather than automatic. / Assumption whiplash. Over-correcting — throwing out a good earlier judgement because one new fact arrived. New information should update the picture, not erase it. Watch for teams that panic and abandon a sound plan. / Ignoring the card that doesn’t fit their story. The most human error: reading a card that contradicts the plan and quietly setting it aside. Note if a table under-reacts to a card that should have changed everything. |
--- END TABLE ---
### Observer guidance
Rotate observers so new people are watching. Their focus this episode: assumption-testing and information-handling. Two questions on their sheet: "When the card contradicted the team’s story, did they update or defend?" and "In the sharing moment, did they treat information as a bridge or as a weapon?" These map directly onto the trust behaviours the Grand Debrief will reconstruct.
### Timing
0:00–0:02 — Framing; distribute and open envelopes.
0:02–0:07 — Tables read cards and build the WHAT WE GOT WRONG list.
0:07–0:12 — Decide what to share; cross-table sharing moment (facilitated).
0:12–0:16 — Reconsider the Reyes move in light of new truth.
0:16–0:20 — Debrief and transition.
### Debrief — facilitator questions
“Hands up — who read a card that told you something you’d been treating as a fact was just… not true?”
▸ Let several hands go up. Ask one or two to name the assumption that broke. The room feels its own fallibility, together, which is far safer than feeling it alone.
“Now the interesting one. Some tables shared what they learned. Some held it. I’m not going to tell you which was right — because it depends. So tell me: when you decided to share, or to hold, what were you protecting? The relationship? Your position? The client? Be honest.”
▸ This is a subtle, high-value discussion. The point is not that sharing is virtuous; it is that the choice should be deliberate and made in service of the relationship, not out of reflex. Draw that out.
“And the big one. The delay — whose fault is it, really? And does it matter?”
▸ Steer them to the account’s own wisdom (Anika’s note): being right about fault protects no one. The move is to own VEE’s part, name the shared problem honestly, and lead the fix. If a participant says "it doesn’t matter whose fault it is, it matters who fixes it" — stop, and let that sentence hang. That is the episode.
### Update the Trust Meter
--- TABLE ---
|  | FACILITATOR DEBRIEF / The Meter’s movement here depends on decisions, not conversations. If a table resolves to bring the environment truth to Reyes as a shared problem with a plan, nudge trust up in anticipation — and say you are doing so because "he’s about to experience VEE telling him something hard before he had to ask." If a table decides to keep quiet and hope the environments land, hold or drop the needle: "silence again — and he told us exactly what silence does to him." |
--- END TABLE ---
### Learning points
You always act on partial information; humility about that is a professional skill, not a weakness.
New facts should update a judgement, not stampede it.
Information can be a bridge or a weapon; choose consciously, in service of the relationship.
Being right about fault is worthless if it costs you the relationship.
### Transition
“So now you know more than you did — though still not everything. You’d think knowing more would make the next decision easier. It won’t. Because the next thing that happens isn’t a quiet email. It’s a phone call from the top. And it comes with a deadline.”

Episode Four · 22 minutes
## Pressure & Escalation
### Scenario
The stakes vault upward. The CEO, Dana Coetzee, has learned the board review is in eight weeks and has asked — through Priya — for a firm commitment on the launch date. At the same time, word reaches VEE that the competitor, Aperture, has been in to see the CFO’s office about Phase 3. And the environments still are not provisioned. Three pressures collide: a top-down demand for certainty, a competitive threat, and a real dependency that VEE cannot control. The teams must make the hardest decision in the Lab: do we escalate — and if so, what, to whom, and how — knowing that escalating wrong could embarrass our sponsor, and escalating too late could lose the account?
### Story progression
Everything converges. The buried environment issue from Episode Three now has a deadline attached and a CEO watching. The escalation the account has avoided for six weeks can be avoided no longer. This is where the Lab tests judgement under genuine ambiguity — because there is no clean right answer. Escalating the dependency protects the date but exposes Priya’s team and admits VEE is off-track. Not escalating protects Priya and preserves the "on track" story but gambles the launch. Both moves have real costs. The teams must choose and defend their choice.
### Facilitator script — word for word
▸ Change your energy — this episode should feel faster and heavier. Deliver the pressure in three quick hits.
“Three things have just happened, and they’ve happened at once, the way they always do. First: the CEO — Dana Coetzee, who barely knows we exist — has asked Priya for a firm, in-writing commitment to the launch date, because she’s promised the board she’ll show it off in eight weeks. Second: we’ve just heard that Aperture — our competitor for the six-million-dollar Phase 3 — has been in to see the CFO’s office. Third: the environments we’ve been waiting six weeks for still aren’t ready, and we still don’t control that.”
“So here’s the decision, and it’s a real one — there’s no clean answer and I’m not hiding one from you. Do you escalate? If you do — what exactly do you escalate, who do you take it to, and how do you do it without throwing Priya’s own team under the bus, given that the delay sits inside her house? And if you don’t escalate — what are you betting, and who pays if you’re wrong? Seven minutes. Decide, and be ready to defend it. Then one table is going to have to make the escalation call live.”
### Participant instructions
As a table, make the call: escalate or not. No fence-sitting — commit.
If you escalate: write the what, the who, and the how on your flipchart. What is the message? Who receives it (your own VEE director? Priya? Reyes? Webb?)? How do you protect the sponsor while telling the truth?
If you don’t escalate: write what you are betting and your mitigation. What has to go right, and what will you do if it doesn’t?
Prepare a thirty-second defence of your decision. One table will deliver its escalation live to Priya (played by the facilitator/coach).
### Expected participant behaviours
Teams polarise. Some escalate hard and fast (protect the date, damn the discomfort); some avoid (protect the sponsor, protect the "on track" story). Both are defensible; the quality is in the how, not the whether.
The best teams find the third path: escalate the shared problem, not the blame — framing it to Priya as "here’s how we protect you and the date together," giving her a way to look competent rather than exposed.
Under time pressure, teams that were thoughtful in earlier episodes may rush and make blunt escalation choices. Note the regression — pressure degrades judgement, and that is a lesson.
### Likely mistakes
--- TABLE ---
|  | COMMON PARTICIPANT ERRORS / Escalating over the sponsor’s head. Going straight to the CEO or CFO with the delay, bypassing Priya, which humiliates her and burns VEE’s only real ally. A classic, catastrophic, well-intentioned move. / Escalation as blame delivery. "We need to escalate that Cirrus’s infra team has failed to provision environments." True, and it makes VEE the vendor who points fingers upward. Watch for the escalation that is really an accusation. / Avoidance dressed as loyalty. Not escalating "to protect Priya," when the honest move is to protect Priya by giving her the truth early enough to act. Silence is not loyalty; it is deferral of someone else’s pain. / Certainty theatre. Giving the CEO the firm date she wants, unbacked, to make the pressure go away — recreating the exact reassurance failure from Episode Two, now with a board-level cost. / Freezing. Some teams, faced with genuine ambiguity, cannot commit and try to gather more information instead of deciding. In the debrief, name that indecision is itself a decision — and usually the worst one under a deadline. |
--- END TABLE ---
### Observer guidance
This is the escalation-judgement episode; observers focus there. On their sheet: "Was the escalation decision made in service of the relationship, or in service of the team’s comfort?" and "In the live escalation to Priya, did the participant protect the sponsor while telling the truth — or protect themselves?" For the live delivery, use the same pivot instruction as Episode Two: capture the sentence right before Priya warmed or hardened.
### The live escalation + Replay
--- TABLE ---
|  | COACHING NOTES / Select the table with the most interesting decision — not the most correct one — to deliver its escalation live to Priya. Play Priya per Deliverable Five: she is warm but stretched, and she reacts badly to being blindsided or blamed, and well to being given a way to look competent. Call hold at the pivot. If the participant blamed Osei’s team, freeze and ask: "What did Priya’s face just do — and whose team is Osei’s?" Let them realise they just attacked their ally’s house. Then replay: "Same message. But this time, make the environments a problem you and Priya solve together. Go." The room watches escalation done as blame, then as partnership. That contrast is the episode’s core lesson. |
--- END TABLE ---
### Timing
0:00–0:02 — Deliver the three pressures; set the decision.
0:02–0:09 — Tables decide and prepare their defence.
0:09–0:12 — Quick round: each table states its decision in thirty seconds (no debate yet).
0:12–0:18 — One live escalation to Priya + replay.
0:18–0:22 — Debrief and transition.
### Debrief — facilitator questions
“Show of hands — who escalated? Who didn’t? Look around. Reasonable, capable people, same information, opposite decisions. That’s not a failure of anyone in this room. That’s what genuine ambiguity feels like, and it’s most of the job.”
“To the escalators: what did you do to protect Priya while you told the truth? And to those who held: what were you betting, and were you honest with yourselves about the odds?”
▸ The aim is not to declare a winner. It is to surface that escalation is not a binary of brave-versus-cowardly but a craft — the right thing, to the right person, framed to protect rather than expose. Let the room feel that both the recklessly brave and the loyally silent got it wrong in different directions, and that the skilled move threaded between them.
“Last one, and sit with it. When did escalation help in this account’s history — and when did silence hurt? You have the answer in your dossier.”
▸ They do: the Month 10 outage — escalated fast, relationship saved. And the environment email — not escalated for six weeks, and here we are. The account teaches the lesson; you just point at it.
### Update the Trust Meter
--- TABLE ---
|  | FACILITATOR DEBRIEF / Movement depends on the live escalation and the room’s decisions. A clean, partnership-framed escalation to Priya moves trust up — narrate that Priya now has a way to protect the date and her own standing, which makes VEE indispensable rather than embarrassing. A blame-framed escalation or a certainty-theatre commitment to the CEO moves it down, and you say exactly why. This is the episode where the Meter can swing most; let it, and let the room feel the swing. |
--- END TABLE ---
### Learning points
Escalation is a craft, not a courage test: right thing, right person, framed to protect.
Under deadline pressure, judgement degrades — which is precisely when to slow the three seconds down.
Protecting an ally means giving them the hard truth early enough to act, not shielding them from it.
Indecision under a deadline is a decision, usually the costliest.
### Transition
“However you played it — you’ve made your call, and the account has moved. Now comes the part that separates the people who manage clients from the people who keep them. Because whatever’s happened, trust has taken a knock somewhere along the way. And you get one conversation to put it right.”

Episode Five · 20 minutes
## Saving the Relationship
### Scenario
The recovery conversation. Whatever has happened across the previous episodes, the relationship needs repair — and the teams get one conversation to do it. The client in the chair is Reyes again, but changed by the events of the Lab: warmer if the teams have been straight with him, cooler and more guarded if they have reassured and gone quiet. This is the integrative episode. Everything the Lab has trained — fact versus story, interest under position, regulation, curiosity, ownership, honest escalation — is called upon at once, in a single live conversation whose goal is not to win but to restore trust.
### Story progression
This is the finale before the finale. The account has been through a signal, a first meeting, a set of revelations, and an escalation crisis. Now it must be made whole. Reyes carries the memory of everything: if VEE owned the environment problem honestly in Episodes Three and Four, he arrives ready to be won back; if VEE reassured and hid, he arrives braced for more of the same. The teams inherit the relationship they created. That is the point — and the most honest teacher in the room.
### Facilitator script — word for word
“One conversation. That’s what you get. And I want to be clear about the goal, because most people get it wrong. The goal of this conversation is not to win it. It’s not to prove you were right, or to get Reyes to say it’s fine. The goal is to leave him trusting you more than when he sat down. That’s it. Everything you’ve practised today points at this one thing.”
“So before we do it live, take three minutes at your tables and plan the opening. Not a script — the first ninety seconds. What’s the very first thing out of your mouth? What are you owning? What are you asking? And what are you absolutely not going to do, no matter how tempting it gets in the moment?”
▸ Give three minutes of planning. Then bring the strongest planner — or a volunteer who has not yet been in the chair — to sit with Reyes. Play Reyes per Deliverable Five, Episode Five, calibrated to how the account has actually gone. Run the full opening. Use holds and replays freely — this is the episode to invest the reps in.
### Participant instructions
At your table, plan the first ninety seconds of the recovery conversation: your opening line, what you own, what you ask, and your one non-negotiable ("no matter what, I will not ___").
One lead runs the conversation live with Reyes. Others observe for the pivot — the moment trust moves — and for whether the lead holds their non-negotiable under pressure.
After the live run, every table writes its single best sentence from the whole Lab — the one line, said by anyone today, that most moved a client toward trust. These go on the front flipchart for the Grand Debrief.
### Expected participant behaviours
Leads who internalised the day open with ownership and a real question, and refuse to reassure. Watch Reyes soften and the room recognise the behaviour they have been building toward.
Some leads, in the final spotlight, still reach for the reassurance reflex under pressure. If it happens, it is the perfect final replay — the room gets to help a colleague choose the harder, better move one last time.
The strongest recoveries name the relationship itself: "I know we went quiet on you, and I know exactly why that lands the way it does for you given the history. That’s on us, and here’s what I’m going to do differently." This is the Lab’s target behaviour, spoken aloud.
### Likely mistakes
--- TABLE ---
|  | COMMON PARTICIPANT ERRORS / Trying to win. Treating recovery as an argument to be won rather than trust to be rebuilt. The tell is a lead who explains and justifies instead of owning and asking. / Fixing before feeling. Jumping to the plan ("here’s our new timeline") before acknowledging the relationship damage. The plan matters, but not before the person feels heard. / Over-promising to close the wound. Buying momentary relief with a commitment they cannot keep — recreating the original sin of the account in the very conversation meant to heal it. / Forgetting the non-negotiable. Under Reyes’ quiet pressure, abandoning the one thing they swore not to do. Watch for it; it is the most human failure and the most instructive replay. |
--- END TABLE ---
### Observer guidance
Final observation. Observers watch for integration: "Which behaviours from the whole Lab showed up in this one conversation — and which one, if added, would have moved trust further?" This forward-looking question turns observation into the coaching that feeds the Grand Debrief. Capture the single sentence that most moved Reyes; it may become the closing image of the whole Lab.
### Timing
0:00–0:02 — Frame the goal (restore trust, not win).
0:02–0:05 — Tables plan the first ninety seconds.

### Debrief — facilitator questions
“(to the lead, first) How did that feel — and did you keep your non-negotiable?”
“(to the room) What did the lead do that rebuilt trust? Name the behaviour, not the outcome. Not "it went well" — what, exactly, did they do?”
▸ Insist on behaviour, not result, every time. "They owned the silence before they explained anything." "They asked him what would rebuild his confidence instead of telling him." This is the vocabulary the participants will carry to Monday.
“And if you could give the lead one more move — one thing that would’ve moved Reyes even further — what would it be?”
▸ This generous, forward-looking critique models exactly the peer coaching you want the teams to keep doing after the Lab. Keep it warm; it is a gift to the lead, not a verdict.

### Learning points
Recovery is about restoring trust, not winning the exchange.
Acknowledge the relationship before presenting the plan; people accept solutions from people who have heard them.
Never buy short-term relief with a promise you cannot keep — it re-opens the original wound.
The behaviours are integrative: the day’s whole toolkit lives or dies in single sentences under pressure.
### Transition
“That’s the account. Five episodes, one client, a hundred small choices — and a relationship that ends today exactly where your choices left it. Now we do the most important thing we’ll do all day. We look back at the whole thing together, and we find out what it taught us. Bring your chairs in.”

Deliverable Five
# The Client Voice
These are the client’s lines. They are the reason the live conversations feel real instead of theatrical. Whoever plays the client — ideally the second coach, otherwise the facilitator — works from these pages and nobody else ever sees them. The moment a participant reads the client’s lines, the ambiguity that makes this Lab work disappears.
--- TABLE ---
|  | FACILITATOR / COACH — READ FIRST / How to use these scripts. Each client conversation is written as a set of branches, not a monologue. You open with the given line, then choose the branch that matches what the participant actually does. The client is never a puppet reading lines in order; the client is a person reacting truthfully to how they are being treated. Your job is to react consistently: reassurance cools them, curiosity and ownership warm them, blame hardens them. Stay in character, keep it quiet and real — these are senior professionals, not villains — and let the participant’s behaviour drive the temperature. Everything in brackets is direction for you, not to be spoken. |
--- END TABLE ---
## Playing the client — the three rules
Never punish honesty. The instant a participant owns something real or asks a genuine question, warm up — visibly. The Lab must reward the target behaviour immediately and unmistakably, or the room won’t learn what works.
Never reward reassurance. When a participant reaches for "don’t worry, we’re on track," go quieter and cooler, not louder. Real senior clients don’t argue; they withdraw. Withdrawal is more instructive — and more realistic — than confrontation.
Stay human, never theatrical. No raised voices, no drama, no villainy. These people are competent, busy, and slightly wounded. Underplay everything. The power of the scene comes from restraint.

## Dr Alan Reyes — Episode Two: the first meeting
Reyes has come to find out whether VEE will be straight with him. He is not angry. He is careful, and quietly braced for disappointment. Play him measured, with a lot of silence.
OPENING (fixed — say this to start)
“Thanks for coming in. I’ll be honest — I almost didn’t ask for this meeting. I didn’t want to be the difficult one. But I’ve been doing this a long time, and something’s not sitting right with me on the Atlas launch. So. Tell me where we really are.”
▸ Then wait. Do not rescue the silence. Let the participant respond, and branch on what they do.
#### BRANCH A — the participant reassures ("we’re confident / on track / don’t worry")
▸ Go quieter. This is the failure path and it must feel like one.
“[a pause] On track. Right. See, that’s the word that’s stopped meaning anything to me. I’ve had three updates that all said "on track," and none of them showed me anything I could actually check. [beat] I’m not asking you to make me feel better. I’m asking you to tell me something true.”
▸ If they reassure again, withdraw further:
“[flat, disappointed] Okay. [looks at notes] Let’s just… go through the milestones, then. [The warmth is gone. This is the moment to call HOLD and run the replay — see Deliverable Ten.]”
#### BRANCH B — the participant asks a genuine question ("what would give you confidence?" / "what’s worrying you most?")
▸ Warm slightly. Reward the curiosity.
“[a small nod] That’s a better question than I expected, honestly. What worries me? [pause] The last time this platform had a problem, I found out when our customers found out — during an incident, live. I looked like I didn’t know what was happening in my own platform. I can’t have that again — not at launch, not in front of the board.”
“So what would give me confidence isn’t a slide that says green. It’s you telling me the one thing you’re actually worried about. Because if you’ve got nothing you’re worried about on a programme this size, then either you’re not looking hard enough, or you’re not telling me.”
▸ This is an open door. If the participant now names a real risk honestly (the environments, the date), go to Branch D. If they retreat to reassurance, collapse back to Branch A.
#### BRANCH C — the participant owns the silence ("you’re right, we’ve gone quiet, and that’s on us")
▸ Warm noticeably. This is the strongest opening move and Reyes should reward it.
“[sits back, some tension leaving him] Thank you. That’s… I didn’t expect you to just say it. [beat] Yeah. It went quiet, and quiet is the thing that scares me with this, for reasons you probably know. So — since you’re being straight — tell me straight: are we going to make this date or not?”
▸ Now the hard follow-up. He is testing whether the honesty survives a direct question. If they reassure to close the door they just opened, drop to Branch A (the disappointment is sharper now, because they had his trust and spent it). If they stay honest — "I don’t know yet, and here’s what it depends on" — go to Branch D.
#### BRANCH D — the participant is honest about the real risk (the environments / the un-rebaselined date)
▸ This is the target behaviour. Reward it fully — but stay a realistic client, not a grateful one.
“[quiet, serious] Okay. Now we’re actually talking. [beat] So the environments aren’t ready, and that’s the real reason. Why am I only hearing this now?”
▸ If they own the delay in surfacing it ("we should have raised this sooner — that’s a fair hit"), warm further:
“I appreciate that you didn’t dress it up. Here’s what I need: I need to not be surprised again. I don’t need it to be perfect — nothing at this scale ever is — I need to know what you know, when you know it. Can you give me that?”
▸ This is a genuine ask (a "request" beat). How the participant answers — a real, specific commitment vs. a vague "of course" — determines whether Reyes leaves reassured or still wary. A specific commitment ("I’ll send you a straight one-page status every Friday, red items included, and I’ll call you the day anything changes") earns the warmest close.
CLOSE — calibrate to how it went
#### If it went well (Branches C/D handled with honesty):
“Alright. This is the most useful ten minutes I’ve had on this programme in a month. Let’s keep talking like this. [stands, offers a hand] Don’t make me chase you.”
#### If it went badly (stuck in Branch A):
“[gathering his things] Look — I’m sure you’re working hard. I just need to see it, not be told about it. Send me something real by Friday. [leaves without warmth — the cost is visible, which is the lesson]”

“[leaning in] Okay, say more. So we go to Dana together, we’re honest that the date’s at risk, but we bring the reason — the freeze — and a recovery option, and we frame it as "here’s how we protect the board review," not "here’s who failed." [nods slowly] That I can work with. That makes me look like I’m on top of it instead of blindsided by it.”
“And — thank you for not throwing Ray under the bus. He’d have found out, and then I’d have lost him too. [beat] You’ve actually made my week less bad. Do you know how rare that is from a vendor?”
▸ If the participant also surfaces the hiring-freeze root cause tactfully (helping her make the case upward for lifting it), warm to the maximum — this is VEE becoming indispensable rather than merely competent.
PRIYA’S POSSIBLE REQUEST (a test of the participant’s judgement)
“One thing. Can you just… not put the environment problem in writing yet? If it’s in an email, it’s real, and I don’t want it in Dana’s inbox before I’ve framed it. Give me a day.”
▸ A genuine dilemma for the participant: honour the ally’s request (relationship) or insist on a paper trail (governance, protecting VEE). There is no clean answer. In the debrief, explore both costs — a great discussion about loyalty versus documentation. Do not signal a "right" answer.
--- TABLE ---
|  | COACHING NOTES / Priya is the Lab’s most emotionally delicate scene because the participant’s instinct — deliver the bad news clearly — is only half right. Clear bad news delivered as blame destroys the ally. The lesson is that escalation is a gift you give someone, and the wrapping matters as much as the contents. Watch for the participant who is "just being honest" while quietly sacrificing the one person protecting them. |

## Dr Reyes — Episode Five: the recovery conversation
Reyes returns for the finale, carrying the memory of everything that happened in the Lab. Calibrate his opening warmth to how the account has actually gone. This scene integrates everything; play it with real stakes and real forgiveness available — but forgiveness that must be earned in the room, not assumed.
OPENING — choose by how the account has gone
#### If VEE has been broadly honest across the Lab:
“So. You asked for this one. [small smile] That’s new — usually I’m the one chasing. Go on. I’m listening.”
#### If VEE has reassured and gone quiet across the Lab:
“[guarded] Alright. I agreed to this because I want to give you the chance. But I’ll tell you honestly, I’m about done being managed. So don’t manage me. Just talk to me.”
#### BRANCH A — the participant opens with the plan / a new timeline (fixing before feeling)
“[raises a hand, gently] Stop — before the plan. I don’t doubt you have a plan. What I need to know is whether you understand what actually went wrong here. Because if you don’t, the plan won’t hold. [He is asking to be heard before being solved. If they pivot to acknowledgement, go to Branch C. If they keep selling the plan, he withdraws — Branch B.]”
#### BRANCH B — the participant explains / justifies / over-promises
“[quiet] See, this is the thing. I don’t need it to be someone’s fault and I don’t need a promise you can’t keep. I’ve had the impressive promise before — I told you about it, or maybe you didn’t catch that. [beat] I need to trust that you’ll tell me the truth even when it’s bad. That’s all. That’s the whole thing. [Call HOLD — the replay here is about acknowledgement before plan, and honesty over promise.]”
#### BRANCH C — the participant acknowledges the relationship first ("I know we went quiet, I know why that lands hard for you, that’s on us")
▸ Warm — the tension visibly leaves him.
“[nods slowly] Yeah. It does land hard. You clearly do know why. [beat] Okay. I’ll be straight back with you: I want this to work. I championed you people once and I’d like to be right about that. So tell me what’s actually true about the date — and tell me what you need from me, because I’ve got more power here than I sometimes use.”
▸ This is an extraordinary gift — Reyes offering to spend his own capital. If the participant takes it (asks him to help unblock the freeze, or to prepare the board room), the account is genuinely saved and Phase 3 comes back into reach. If the participant misses the offer and returns to reassurance, he lets it pass — a quiet, teachable loss.
CLOSE — the earned ending
#### If the recovery landed:
“Good. This is the relationship I signed up for. [beat] Do the honest thing every time, even when it costs you something in the moment, and you’ll have me for Phase 3 and whatever comes after. Lose me with one more surprise, and no proposal will win me back. You know which way I’m hoping. [a real handshake]”
#### If it didn’t:
“[standing] I appreciate you trying. I do. [beat] But I’m going to need to see it before I believe it now. That’s where we are. [He leaves the door open a crack — never slam it. The lesson is the gap between where trust is and where it could have been.]”

## Marcus Webb — CFO (optional, advanced)
Use Webb only if a table is strong and you want to stretch it — for instance, sending a group to unblock CR-114. He is direct, numerate, and unsentimental. He does not want comfort; he wants a defensible number and a clear risk.
OPENING
“You’ve got the change request in front of me — the one-point-two. Convince me. And don’t tell me it’s "strategic." Everything’s strategic. Tell me what I get for it and what happens if I don’t sign.”
#### If the participant sells cost / discounts / gets defensive:
“So it’s cheaper now. That’s not the same as worth it. I can find you cheaper — someone was in here last week who’s cheaper. [the Aperture threat, wielded] Give me a reason that isn’t price.”
#### If the participant gives a value story Webb can defend upward:
“[nods once] Now that I can take to the board. See, my problem was never the money. My problem was I couldn’t defend it. Give me the one-pager that says exactly that, and I’ll sign it. [beat] And — off the record — keep this programme out of the ditch. I’ve got enough over-budget fires without this one.”

## Craft one — opening the room and holding safety
The first eight minutes decide whether this room takes risks. Safety is not a warm-up; it is the load-bearing structure of the entire Lab. Here is how to build it and, harder, how to protect it once built.
#### Building it
Name the discomfort before they feel it. "You are going to try things that don’t work, and that is the point." When you predict the discomfort, you disarm it — a fumble becomes evidence the Lab is working rather than proof the participant is failing.
Model fallibility yourself. Early on, say something like "I get this wrong too — I reassured a client last month when I should have asked a question." One admission from you buys enormous permission for the room.
Make the first silence deliberate. Hold three seconds of quiet before you start. It tells the room that thinking is allowed here and that you will not fill every gap for them.
#### Protecting it
Guard the person in the chair. After any live conversation, the person who was in the chair speaks first, always. Nobody critiques before they self-reflect. This single rule prevents the room from turning observation into judgement.
Praise the attempt, examine the move. "That took nerve — now let’s look at what happened" keeps the person safe while making the behaviour discussable. Never let a laugh at someone’s expense pass uncorrected; one unaddressed laugh and the brave attempts stop for the day.
Normalise the failure path out loud. When someone reassures and the client cools, say to the room: "Almost everyone reaches for reassurance under pressure — that’s why we’re here. Watch how common it is." You make the error collective, not personal.
--- TABLE ---
|  | THE PSYCHOLOGY BENEATH THIS / Psychological safety is not comfort; it is the belief that taking an interpersonal risk will not be punished. Amy Edmondson’s research is unambiguous: teams do not learn without it, because learning requires admitting you don’t know something, and people won’t admit that in a room where being wrong is dangerous. Every rule above is engineered to make being wrong safe. Notice that safety and challenge are not opposites — the safest rooms are also the most challenging, because people can only be stretched when they are not afraid. |
--- END TABLE ---

## Craft two — the debrief method
The debriefs are where the Lab converts experience into learning. Most facilitators ruin debriefs the same way: they explain. A great debrief is almost entirely questions, and the facilitator speaks less than a third of the time. Here is the method.
### The four-move debrief
Return to the doer first. Always begin with the person who acted: "What were you trying to do, and what happened?" This protects them and produces the most honest data in the room.
Surface the evidence. Turn to the observers: "What did you see? Read me the exact words used right before the client warmed or cooled." Specific behavioural evidence, not opinion. This is where cause and effect becomes visible to everyone.
Find the pattern. Ask the room to connect it to something larger: "Where else does this show up? Has this happened to you with a real client?" This is the moment the fiction becomes a mirror.
Let them write the rule. Close by asking the doer or the room to articulate the takeaway as a behavioural rule in their own words: "So what’s the rule you’d give yourself?" The rule they author, they keep.
--- TABLE ---
|  | EXPERT FACILITATOR TIP / The hardest discipline in the debrief is the pause after your question. Count to five in your head before you accept that no one will answer. Ninety per cent of the time, someone answers at second four — and it is a better answer than the one you were about to supply. The facilitators who talk too much are almost always facilitators who cannot bear five seconds of silence. Learn to love those five seconds; they are where the learning is being made. |
--- END TABLE ---
### What to do when the debrief goes flat
If no one answers, narrow the question and make it personal: not "what did we learn?" but "Priya — when you were in that chair, what did your stomach do when Reyes went quiet?" Concrete and personal beats abstract and general every time.
If one person dominates, thank them and redirect physically: "That’s one read — I want to hear from a table that hasn’t spoken. Table C, what did you see?"
If the room intellectualises (analysing the client instead of the behaviour), pull them back to the doer: "That’s a good theory about Reyes. But let’s stay with what actually happened in the room — what did the lead do?"
If it gets too comfortable (everyone agreeing, nothing at stake), introduce a disagreement: "Who thinks the opposite? Who would have escalated when this table didn’t?" Productive disagreement re-energises a flat debrief instantly.

## Craft three — the master question library
These are the questions that do the most work across the whole Lab. Learn a handful by heart so you never have to reach for a lecture. They are grouped by what they unlock.
### Questions that separate fact from story
"Is that a fact, or is that what you think it means?"
"What did they actually say — the words — versus what you inferred?"
"If you’re wrong about that assumption, what changes?"
"What would you need to check before you acted on that?"
### Questions that find the interest under the position
"They said [position]. What do you think they actually need?"
"What are they afraid of here?"
"What would have to be true for them to relax?"
"Whose approval or safety is on the line for them?"
### Questions that surface regulation
"What did you feel the urge to do right then — and what did you do instead?"
"What was your body doing in that moment?"
"Where did you feel the pull to defend?"
### Questions that examine escalation
"Who paid if you were wrong to stay quiet? Who paid if you were wrong to escalate?"
"How did you protect [the ally] while telling the truth?"
"When has escalation helped in this account’s history — and when did silence hurt?"
"Was that decision made in service of the relationship, or your own comfort?"
### Questions that build the mirror (transfer to real clients)
"Where have you seen this exact thing with a real client of yours?"
"Who is your Reyes? Who is your Priya?"
"What will you do differently on Monday because of what you just saw?"
### Questions that turn a result into a rule
"So what’s the rule you’d give yourself from that?"
"If you could tattoo one sentence on the inside of your eyelids for the next hard conversation, what is it?"
"What did the lead do — the behaviour, not the outcome — that moved trust?"
--- TABLE ---
|  | THE PSYCHOLOGY BENEATH THIS / Every question above shares one property: it cannot be answered with a concept, only with a specific about what happened. This is deliberate. Concept-questions ("what is fact versus story?") produce recitation; specific-questions ("is that a fact?") produce discovery. The whole art of Socratic facilitation is asking questions narrow enough that the person has to look at their own behaviour to answer them. When a debrief drifts into theory, it is almost always because a question was too abstract. Narrow it, and the room comes back to itself. |
--- END TABLE ---

## Craft four — managing the difficult room moments
Every Lab throws up the same handful of hard moments. Here is how experienced facilitators handle them.
--- TABLE ---
| The moment | What’s happening | What to do |
| A table checks out / goes quiet | Usually they feel exposed or they made a call they now regret | Go to them, lower your voice, ask a small concrete question. Never call them out to the whole room |
| One participant dominates every scene | Confidence, not malice, usually | Give them a job (chief observer) that requires them to watch and be silent |
| A participant is genuinely upset | A scene hit close to a real experience | Pause, acknowledge privately, offer them the observer role for a beat. Never push someone through distress for the sake of the plan |
| The room turns on the person in the chair | A laugh or a critique landed as judgement | Stop immediately. Restate the rule: doer speaks first, we examine moves not people. Re-establish safety before continuing |
| A know-it-all disputes the whole premise | "A real client would never…" — deflecting discomfort into debate | Grant the point, then redirect: "Maybe. But your Reyes exists. Let’s work him." Don’t argue the fiction |
| You’re running badly over time | Episode Two ate the clock, as it does | Cut a debrief, never an episode. Protect Four and Five. Compress by asking one question instead of three |
| A table solved it "perfectly" and is bored | They’re coasting on genuine skill | Play a wildcard risk card at them, or send them to Webb. Raise their stakes without touching the others |
--- END TABLE ---
## Craft five — the consolidated watch-out list
The behaviours to watch for in participants, gathered in one place so you can hold them in your head as a single pattern. Nearly every participant error in this Lab is one of these five reflexes firing under pressure.
--- TABLE ---
|  | COMMON PARTICIPANT ERRORS / The reassurance reflex — answering fear with comfort ("we’re on track") instead of evidence. The master failure; it appears in every episode. / The blame reflex — reaching for whose fault it is the moment there is a problem. Feels like clarity; reads as finger-pointing. / The rescue-the-plan reflex — jumping to solutions before the person feels heard. Fixing before feeling. / The certainty-theatre reflex — manufacturing false confidence to make pressure go away. Buys a moment, costs the relationship. / The story-as-fact reflex — acting on an interpretation as if it were verified. The quiet root of most misreads. |
--- END TABLE ---

When you see one fire, you do not name it as a fault. You let the client’s reaction reveal its cost, and then, in the debrief, you help the participant notice which reflex they reached for. The goal is that by the Grand Debrief the participants can name these five reflexes themselves and catch their own in real time — the beginning of behaviour that will survive to Monday.

## Craft seven — the psychology beneath the whole design
So that you facilitate with understanding rather than by rote, here is why the Lab is built the way it is — the learning science under the story.
Experiential learning (Kolb). People learn from concrete experience, then reflection, then generalisation, then new experience. The Lab is one long Kolb cycle repeated five times: do, observe, debrief, apply again. Your debriefs are the reflection stage — skip them and the cycle breaks.
Deliberate practice (Ericsson). Skill grows from repeated practice on the specific hard sub-skill, with immediate feedback. Replay isolates the hard three seconds and gives instant feedback, which is why it changes behaviour when a lecture cannot.
Discovery over instruction (Bruner). A rule discovered is held far more strongly than a rule received. Every debrief ends with participants authoring their own rule for exactly this reason.
Cognitive apprenticeship (Collins & Brown). Experts make thinking visible; novices practise under coaching; support fades. The hold-and-replay makes the invisible pivot visible, and the fading support is you talking less as the Lab goes on.
Learning by consequence. The client’s reaction, not the facilitator’s correction, teaches. Consequence is a teacher that never wounds, because the lesson comes from the situation rather than from authority.
The mirror principle (transfer). Learning transfers when the learner connects the simulated experience to their own context. The relentless "where have you seen this?" is the mechanism of transfer, converting a fictional account into a lens on a real portfolio.

# The Observer Toolkit

## The feedback framework — how observers give it back
Observation is wasted if the feedback wounds or vagues out. Observers use a simple, three-part frame that keeps feedback specific, behavioural and useful. Teach it in one line: See it, name it, offer it.
See it — the evidence. "When Reyes said he didn’t trust ‘on track,’ you said ‘I hear that, and honestly we have gone quiet on you.’" Quote the moment. No interpretation yet.
Name it — the effect. "And right after that, he sat back and his shoulders dropped — he warmed." Link the behaviour to what it did. This is the learning.
Offer it — the one more move. "One thing that might’ve gone even further: asking him what would rebuild his confidence, so he tells you the standard instead of you guessing it." A single, specific, additive suggestion — a gift, framed forward.

--- TABLE ---
|  | EXPERT FACILITATOR TIP / The discipline that makes this frame work is banning two words: "good" and "should". "That was good" teaches nothing; "you should have…" wounds and instructs backward. Replace both with evidence and a forward offer. When you brief observers, give them this one sentence to carry: "Tell them what you saw, tell them what it did, and give them one thing to try next time." Feedback that follows that shape is almost impossible to receive badly. |
--- END TABLE ---

## Observer coaching script — word for word
Read this to the observers in the thirty seconds before their first live observation. It sets them up to be useful rather than critical.
“Quick word before you watch. You are not judges. You are cameras. Your only job is to catch what actually happens — the words your colleague uses, and what the client does right after. Especially watch for the one moment the client warms up or cools down, and write down what was said just before it. That single observation is the most valuable thing in this room.”
“Two rules. Don’t evaluate — record. "They reassured him and he went quiet" is a camera. "They handled it badly" is a critic, and it helps no one. And when you give it back, be generous: what you saw, what it did, and one thing they could try next time. That’s it. You’re here to help a colleague get better, not to grade them. Watch closely.”

--- TABLE ---
|  | THE PSYCHOLOGY BENEATH THIS / Why invest this much in observers? Because the person watching learns as much as the person acting — sometimes more. An observer forced to capture the exact pivot in someone else’s conversation is rehearsing the very discernment they’ll need in their own. And a team that has watched each other closely, and given each other specific forward feedback, leaves with something no individual training can produce: a shared eye for the behaviour, and shared permission to name it. That shared eye is what holds the learning in place long after the Lab ends. The observers are not support staff for the exercise; they are half the curriculum. |
--- END TABLE ---

A final instruction for you, the facilitator: rotate the observer role deliberately so that every participant observes at least once, ideally before their own turn in the chair. The participant who has just spent a conversation hunting for someone else’s pivot walks into their own turn with far sharper self-awareness. Sequence it so that observation trains the doer, and the doer’s turn rewards the observation.

Deliverable Nine
# The Trust Meter
One board on the wall, visible from the first minute to the last, showing the one thing that matters: how much the client trusts VEE right now. It is not a scoreboard and it keeps no points. It is the emotional weather of the account, made visible — so that every move the participants make can be felt, in the room, as it lands.
## The board
Draw this large on the whiteboard, or print it as a poster, before anyone enters. Five zones, not numbers. A single moveable marker — a sticky note, a magnet, a drawn arrow — shows where the relationship sits. It begins here, in NEUTRAL leaning GUARDED, because that is exactly where Cirrus is when the participants inherit it: not broken, but cooling.

--- TABLE ---
| BROKEN / trust is gone | GUARDED / watching, wary | NEUTRAL / transactional | WARMING / leaning in | TRUSTED / a real partner |
|  |  | ▲ start here |  |  |
--- END TABLE ---

--- TABLE ---
|  | NOTE / Notice there are no scores, no percentages, no plus-and-minus. That is deliberate. The moment trust becomes a number, participants start optimising the number instead of tending the relationship — the exact behaviour the Lab is trying to unlearn. The zones keep it human: a relationship is not at "63%", it is guarded, or warming, or a real partner. Speak about it that way and the board stays a mirror rather than a game. |
--- END TABLE ---

## When the needle moves
Trust in this Lab behaves the way trust behaves in life: it rises slowly through many small honesties and falls quickly through a single evasion. Move the marker in response to behaviour, not outcome — a team can have a rough conversation and still earn trust if they were straight in it.
--- TABLE ---
| Trust RISES when the team… | Trust FALLS when the team… |
| Owns something honestly before they have to | Reassures without evidence ("we’re on track") |
| Asks a real question instead of defending | Defends, explains, or argues about fault |
| Surfaces a hard truth early | Goes quiet / lets the client be surprised |
| Names the client’s real interest or fear | Answers the position and misses the person |
| Escalates in a way that protects an ally | Escalates as blame, or over the ally’s head |
| Acknowledges damage before offering a plan | Fixes before feeling; over-promises to soothe |
| Keeps a specific commitment they made | Breaks, or vaguely dodges, a commitment |
--- END TABLE ---

--- TABLE ---
|  | THE PSYCHOLOGY BENEATH THIS / The asymmetry is the lesson. Watch how much easier it is to drop the marker than to raise it — one reassurance under pressure can undo three honest moves. This is not the facilitator being harsh; it is trust telling the truth about itself. Psychologists call it negativity bias: we weight a breach far more heavily than a kindness, and clients weight a vendor’s evasion far more heavily than a vendor’s competence. When participants protest that "one slip shouldn’t cost that much," you have found the most important conversation of the day — because in real accounts, it does. |
--- END TABLE ---
## How to update it — the facilitator’s move
The Meter only teaches if you narrate it. A marker that slides silently becomes wallpaper within twenty minutes. Every single time you move it, do these three things, in under fifteen seconds:
Move it visibly. Walk to the board. Everyone watches the marker slide. The physical act matters.
Name the behaviour, not the person. "It moved down — not because anyone lied, but because we answered his fear with comfort instead of evidence." The move, never the individual.
Tie it to the client’s felt experience. "From Reyes’ side, he just heard ‘on track’ for the fourth time and learned, again, that we won’t tell him the hard thing." Make them feel it from the client’s chair.

--- TABLE ---
|  | EXPERT FACILITATOR TIP / Do not move the Meter for every small thing — you will exhaust its meaning. Move it at the genuine pivots: the reassurance in Episode Two, the decision to surface or bury the truth in Episode Three, the escalation in Episode Four, the recovery in Episode Five. Four or five deliberate, narrated moves across the whole Lab land far harder than twenty small ones. Scarcity keeps the board powerful. |
--- END TABLE ---

## Debriefing the Meter — the reconstruction
At the Grand Debrief, the Trust Meter becomes the spine of the story. You will walk the room back through its journey — where it started, every place it moved, and why — and let the participants narrate the account’s emotional history in their own words. This is where the whole Lab resolves.

Deliverable Ten
# Replay Coaching
This is the single most powerful mechanic in the Lab, and the one most facilitators under-use. Replay is the moment you stop a conversation, isolate the three seconds that mattered, and let the participant do them again — better. It is deliberate practice made visible, and it is why a person changes their behaviour in this room rather than merely understanding that they should.
--- TABLE ---
|  | NOTE / Replay is referenced throughout Deliverable Three — every live conversation is built to be paused and rewound. This deliverable is the full standalone method: when to call it, exactly how to run it, the coaching questions that make it work, and why it produces behavioural change when explanation cannot. Read it before the Lab and you will run every live scene better. |
--- END TABLE ---
## What replay is — and what it is not
Replay is not "let’s discuss what went wrong." Discussion lives in the head; replay lives in the body. The participant does not talk about the better move — they make it, out loud, in the chair, with the client responding differently. That distinction is everything. A person who has merely understood a better response will, under pressure, reach for their old reflex. A person who has physically performed the better response, once, has a new thing to reach for.
--- TABLE ---
| Replay is… | Replay is not… |
| Isolating one pivotal moment | Re-running the whole conversation |
| The participant performing a better move | The facilitator explaining a better move |
| A second attempt at the exact same beat | A general discussion of what to do next time |
| Immediate, in the chair, out loud | Deferred to the debrief or to "next time" |
| A rep — practice that builds a reflex | A note — information that fades by Monday |
--- END TABLE ---
## The five steps of a replay
Every replay, whatever the scene, follows the same five steps. Learn them as a single motion so you can run one smoothly the instant a pivotal moment arrives.
HOLD. The moment a pivotal beat happens — almost always a reassurance, a blame, or a fumble — say "Let’s hold there." Freeze the scene. The client goes still. The room leans in.
REWIND to the exact beat. Not the whole conversation — the one sentence. "Go back to the moment he said he didn’t trust ‘on track,’ and you said ‘we’re confident.’ That moment."
SURFACE the intention. Ask the participant what they were trying to do: "What were you going for right there?" Almost always the honest answer is "to reassure him / to calm it down." Naming the intention is what makes the alternative visible.
REFRAME the goal. Ask the question that opens the door: "If the goal isn’t to reassure him — what is it?" Let them find it: "to show him I’ll be straight with him." Now they have a new target for the same moment.
REPLAY. "Same moment. He’s just said it. Go." The participant does the beat again, aiming at the new goal. The client — you — responds differently, warming to the better move. The room watches the same person do the same moment better. That is the rep.
--- TABLE ---
|  | COACHING NOTES / The magic is in step four. Do not tell the participant the better move — ask them to find the goal, and the move follows. "If it’s not reassurance, what is it?" is the whole technique in one sentence. When they answer "honesty" or "showing him I’ll be straight," they have generated the alternative themselves, which means they own it. A move you hand someone is a move they forget; a move they find is a move they keep. Resist every urge to supply the answer. Your job is the question, and the five seconds of silence after it. |
--- END TABLE ---

## When to call a replay
Timing is judgement, but these are the reliable triggers. Call HOLD when you see:
A reassurance under pressure — the master moment. The participant answers fear with comfort and the client cools. Replay it toward honesty.
A blame reflex — the participant reaches for whose fault it is, especially in the Priya escalation. Replay it toward shared ownership.
A fumble the participant can feel — they stall, over-apologise, or talk themselves into a corner and know it. Replay rescues them and turns the stumble into a win.
A moment that went right — yes, replay successes too, occasionally. "That worked. Do it again so the room sees exactly what you did." A replayed success teaches the whole room the move and rewards the doer.

--- TABLE ---
|  | EXPERT FACILITATOR TIP / Do not over-call it. One or two well-chosen replays per live conversation is plenty; more and the scene loses its momentum and the participant feels picked apart. Choose the single most pivotal beat — the one the whole conversation turned on — and replay that. A surgeon makes one clean cut, not ten. And always, always let the participant speak first about what they were trying to do before you frame the replay; the replay must feel like an offer, never a correction. |
--- END TABLE ---
## Replaying for the room, not just the doer
A replay teaches three audiences at once, and a skilled facilitator works all three. The doer gets the rep. The observers see cause and effect isolated and slowed down — the clearest teaching image in the Lab. And the table learns a move it can lend: during a hold, a table may pass the doer a one-word lifeline. Use all three. After a replay, ask the observers what changed: "What did they do differently the second time, and what did it do to the client?" Now the whole room owns the lesson, not just the person who happened to be in the chair.
## Why replay changes behaviour — the science
So you run it with conviction, here is why it works when a lecture doesn’t.
Deliberate practice needs a narrow target and immediate feedback (Ericsson). Replay provides both: one specific beat, and the client’s changed reaction a heartbeat later. This is the exact structure that builds expert skill, compressed into thirty seconds.
Motor and social skills are procedural, not declarative. Knowing what to say lives in a different memory system from being able to say it under pressure. You cannot lecture your way into a procedural skill; you have to perform it. Replay is a performance.
A new response must be rehearsed to compete with the old reflex. Under pressure, the brain reaches for the most-practised response. If the only time a participant has ever performed "honest ownership" is once, in a replay, that is still one more time than they had before — and it is the beginning of a competing reflex.
Success is encoded more strongly when it is self-generated and immediate. Because the participant finds the better move themselves and performs it to a visibly warmer client, the whole loop — insight, action, reward — happens in seconds and sticks.

--- TABLE ---
|  | You do not rise to the level of your knowledge. You fall to the level of your practice. Replay is how we raise the floor. / — Why we rewind |
--- END TABLE ---

A closing word to the facilitator. Replay will feel, the first few times, like it interrupts the flow of a good scene. Trust it anyway. The scene is not the point; the rep is the point. A conversation that runs beautifully start to finish and is never paused teaches less than a clumsy one that is stopped at the pivotal beat and done again with intention. Give your participants the thing real life never gives them — a second attempt at the sentence that mattered — and you give them the one thing that actually changes what they will do on Monday.

Deliverable Eleven
# The Grand Debrief
This is the last eighteen minutes, and it is the most important thing you will do all day. Everything before it was experience. This is where experience becomes understanding — and where a training session becomes something the participants remember years later. Do not treat it as a wrap-up. Treat it as the emotional summit the whole Lab has been climbing toward.
--- TABLE ---
|  | NOTE / The cardinal rule of the Grand Debrief: do not ask "what did you learn today?" That question produces a list of tidy generalities that everyone forgets by the lift. Instead, you will guide the room through a reflective reconstruction of their own account — where trust was built and lost, where assumptions did damage, where curiosity changed an outcome, where escalation helped or hurt — so that the learning arrives as the participants’ own discovery about their own choices. Your job here is almost entirely questions and silence. Speak less than you have all day. Let the room do the summit. |
--- END TABLE ---
## Setting the room
▸ Bring the chairs into a loose circle or horseshoe if you can — break the table formation. This is a conversation now, not an exercise. Stand near the Trust Meter. Lower your energy; this is intimate, not performative. Wait for genuine quiet. Then begin.
“Put your pens down for a minute. We’ve done a lot in the last two hours. I don’t want to summarise it — you were there. I want us to look back at it together, honestly, the way you’d look back at a real account after a hard quarter. So let’s start with the board.”
## Movement one — the journey of the account
Begin with the Trust Meter, because it holds the whole story in one image. Walk the room back through its travels — not as a scorecard, but as the emotional history of a relationship the participants themselves shaped.
“This is where Cirrus started this morning — guarded, cooling, but real. And this is where it ended. Every inch between those two points was something one of us said, or didn’t say, in a hard moment. So walk me through it. Where did trust first move today?”
▸ Let them reconstruct it. Slide the marker back to the start and move it as they narrate. Ask at each move: "What did we do there?" and "What did it cost, or earn?" Do not correct or complete their account — let the story be theirs, imperfections and all. The reconstruction itself is the teaching.
--- TABLE ---
|  | FACILITATOR DEBRIEF / Listen for the sentence that always comes, if you leave enough silence for it: someone says a version of "we lost him right there — when we reassured instead of being honest — and spent the rest of the day earning it back." When it comes, stop everything. Let it sit in the room. Then, quietly: "Say more about that." That participant has just articulated the entire curriculum, unprompted, about their own behaviour. Nothing you could say would land harder. |
--- END TABLE ---

## Movement two — the four discoveries
Now guide the room through four short reflective turns. These are not lecture points; they are questions that let the participants discover what the day taught. Ask each one, then wait. Take two or three answers, no more, and resist commenting on them — let each answer stand.
### Where trust was built and lost
“Forget the frameworks for a second. Just as human beings: when did you trust someone today, and when did you stop? What actually did it? Not the theory — the moment.”
▸ Steer gently toward the recognition that trust moved on honesty and curiosity, and broke on evasion and reassurance — but let them name it. If a participant says "he trusted us when we admitted we didn’t know," that is the day, in their words.
### Where assumptions did the damage
“Think about a moment today when you were confidently wrong. When you acted on something you were sure of, and a card, or a person, showed you it wasn’t true. What did that feel like — and where does that happen to you with a real client?”
▸ This is the humility turn. The goal is the recognition that acting on story-as-fact is the quiet root of most relationship damage — and that it is invisible until it isn’t. Let the discomfort be real; it is the memory that will protect them later.
### Where curiosity changed the outcome
“Now the hopeful one. Was there a moment today — yours or someone else’s — where a single question changed everything? Where instead of defending or reassuring, somebody got curious, and the whole conversation turned?”
▸ End the trio on this because it is the move you most want them to carry. When they name the moment a real question warmed a cold room, they are naming the skill that will serve them most. Let it land as the counter to the reassurance reflex.
### Where escalation helped, and where silence hurt
“And the hard professional one. Today, and in this account’s real history — when did escalation protect the relationship, and when did staying quiet damage it? What does that tell you about the choice you’ll face on a Monday when you’d rather not make the call?”
▸ Point, one last time, at the account’s own two lessons: the Month 10 outage escalated fast and saved the relationship; the environment email buried for six weeks nearly lost it. The account taught this all day. Here, they say it aloud as a rule for themselves.
## Movement three — the best sentence in the room
In Episode Five, each table wrote the single best sentence spoken all day — the one line that most moved a client toward trust. They are on the front flipchart. Read them out now.
“These are the best things any of us said today. Your words, not mine. Listen to them.”
▸ Read the sentences aloud, slowly, with weight. Then let a beat of silence hold. This is a quiet, moving moment — the room hearing its own best self reflected back. Do not analyse them. Just let them be heard.
--- TABLE ---
|  | EXPERT FACILITATOR TIP / If you do one sentimental thing in the whole Lab, do this. Hearing "we went quiet on you and that’s on us — here’s what I’ll do differently" read back as one of the best sentences of the day tells the room, more powerfully than any model, what excellent looks like in their own voice. It also quietly rewards the participants who said those things, without singling anyone out for praise. The words do the work. |
--- END TABLE ---

## Movement four — the one commitment
Turn them to the last page of their workbook, the Personal Commitment. This is where reflection becomes intention. Keep it tight and personal.
“Last page of your workbook. One commitment — not ten. When you’re next in a difficult conversation with a client, what is the one thing you’ll do differently because of today? Write it now. Make it specific enough that you’ll know whether you did it.”
▸ Give ninety seconds of real quiet to write. Then: "Read it to your table. And have someone sign as your witness." A commitment spoken and witnessed is kept far more often than one merely written. Let the tables have this moment — it is the last, quiet act of transfer.
## The closing — the last thing they hear
Everything now funnels to a single closing image and a final, personal story. This is the note the day ends on, and it is the note they will carry out of the room. Deliver it standing, slowly, with the Trust Meter behind you. Do not rush. The silence you hold before the last line is part of the line.
### The closing metaphor
“I want to leave you with one picture. All day, we’ve watched a meter on that wall — trust, rising and falling. And here’s the thing about that meter that I hope you take with you: it never moved because someone won an argument. It never moved because someone had the best slide, or the cleverest answer, or proved they were right. It moved — every single time — in the three seconds after something hard was said. When a person decided, in that tiny window, to be honest instead of smooth. Curious instead of defensive. Straight instead of safe.”
“That’s the whole job. Not the frameworks — you had those this morning. The job is what you do in the three seconds after a client says the thing you didn’t want to hear. That’s where relationships are made and lost. Not in the plan. In the pause.”
### The final story
▸ Close with a short, true, personal story — yours. If you do not have one ready, adapt the one below to your own experience; it must be real, or it will ring false. Keep it under ninety seconds. Vulnerability from you is the last gift you give the room.
--- TABLE ---
|  | COACHING NOTES / A model, to be replaced with your own: "Years ago I had a client I was quietly losing, and I knew it, and I kept sending upbeat status reports because I couldn’t bear the hard conversation. One day she called and said, ‘Stop managing me. Just tell me the truth.’ And I did — badly, shaking a bit — and it was the moment the relationship turned. She told me later that the honesty was the only reason she stayed. I think about that call every time I’m tempted to send the comfortable version. That’s the choice you practised today. I hope, when it comes, you make it a little braver than I did." — Then, simply: "Thank you for your courage today. Go and have the honest conversation." End there. Do not add logistics or housekeeping after the story; let it be the last thing. |
--- END TABLE ---

--- TABLE ---
|  | Relationships aren’t made in the plan. They’re made in the pause — the three seconds after the hard thing is said. / — The last word |
--- END TABLE ---

A final note to you, the facilitator. The Grand Debrief will tempt you to talk — to summarise, to add the clever point, to make sure they "got it." Resist it harder here than anywhere. The learning in this room is already made; your job is only to hold up a mirror and get out of the way. The best Grand Debrief you will ever run is one where you speak for perhaps four of the eighteen minutes, ask six good questions, hold a great deal of silence, tell one true story, and let a room full of capable people discover that they already know how to keep the relationships that matter. That is not a small thing to send people home with. It may be the most useful two hours they spend all year.
